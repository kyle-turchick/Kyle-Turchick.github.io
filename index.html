About

I work at the boundary between ecological theory and land-management decisions.

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Natural Resources at Cornell University and a member of the USGS New York Cooperative Fish & Wildlife Research Unit. My research asks how disturbance and environmental heterogeneity shape biodiversity, vegetation structure, soil-surface conditions, and carbon dynamics. I am especially interested in research that can inform public-land management, renewable-energy development, restoration, and conservation under changing disturbance regimes.

Research program

Three research lines, one ecological question: how does variation become biodiversity?

Heterogeneity-diversity theory

I test how environmental heterogeneity and geodiversity influence plant and bird richness across spatial scales, and whether biodiversity responses are linear, saturating, or unimodal.

Renewable-energy ecology

I evaluate how utility-scale solar development changes desert vegetation, soil-surface structure, biological soil crust, woody debris, burrow activity, plant communities, and geodiversity.

Disturbance and recovery

I study how landslides, wildfire, windstorms, mechanical treatments, and solar-facility construction reorganize vegetation, biodiversity, soil conditions, and carbon stocks.

Featured projects

Field systems as living instruments.

My projects combine field data, public-land monitoring, geospatial synthesis, and ecological theory across desert, temperate rainforest, and western U.S. public-land systems.

Yellow Pine Solar Facility ecological monitoring

Permanent-transect monitoring in the Mojave Desert comparing utility-scale solar-facility disturbance, undisturbed controls, and a landslide/debris-flow reference system.

The project quantifies perennial and annual vegetation, species richness, biological soil crust, woody debris, burrows, rock cover, landforms, slope shape, geodiversity, and photovoltaic-panel microsites.

The work helps evaluate how lower-disturbance solar-site preparation and panel microsites affect ecological recovery in arid public-land systems.

Western U.S. geodiversity-biodiversity synthesis

This project integrates federal vegetation and soil monitoring data, bird richness estimates, geodiversity metrics, geologic and edaphic variables, and ecoregion frameworks across western U.S. public lands.

The goal is to test whether abiotic heterogeneity can help explain biodiversity patterns across management-relevant ecological scales.

Southeast Alaska disturbance-carbon research

My master’s research examined how long-term landslide and windstorm disturbance regimes influence biomass, soils, terrain, and carbon stocks in temperate rainforest systems near Sitka, Alaska.

This work connects disturbance ecology with carbon-cycle questions in forests where rare but severe disturbance can reshape long-term carbon storage.

Methods portfolio

From transects to models.

Vegetation and biodiversity

Perennial and annual belt transects, line-intercept cover, species-richness inventories, demographic classes, flowering status, native/introduced richness, shrub structure, and vegetation response to disturbance.

Soils and surface conditions

Soil sampling, fuel loading, post-fire monitoring, rock clast-size cover, landform and slope-shape classification, biological soil crust, woody debris, burrow-belt surveys, and treatment-effectiveness monitoring.

Analysis and workflows

Mixed-effects models, model comparison, AIC/AICc, spatially balanced sampling, geodiversity metrics, richness metrics, ecoregion-based scale analysis, panel microsite classification, R workflows, and metadata development.
